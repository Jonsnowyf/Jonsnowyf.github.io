About

I am a PhD student in Economics at the National University of Singapore. My fields are labor economics and the economics of artificial intelligence, with macroeconomics and international trade as secondary fields. Research

Firm Organization, Reallocation, and Technological Change

[Draft]  [Hide]

Current evaluations of new technologies such as generative AI often measure cost savings while holding the organization of work fixed. This paper studies what such calculations miss and what can be identified before the technology arrives. In a general equilibrium model with monopolistic competition, entry, and exit, firms divide a chain of tasks among specialized roles. Specialization saves time on individual tasks, but passing work between roles takes time. When a technology lowers the cost of performing tasks outside a role’s specialty, holding organization fixed captures the first-order change in aggregate consumption in the stationary equilibrium. The same calculation can miss first-order changes in employment across firms and in firm entry. For larger shocks, reorganization can also raise consumption and change which firms operate. Current firm data identify the direct gain, but leave the costs of alternative organizations undetermined. Because the division of labor depends on market size, a firm’s past responses to demand reveal how profitable it would be to reorganize. When roles adjust smoothly, these responses identify the local reorganization gain and the first-order employment response. With discrete roles and unrestricted unit costs, a complete demand history identifies the gain if and only if no previously unchosen organization could outperform the best previously chosen one after the shock. Finite demand histories yield sharp bounds.

Infrastructure Financing, Fiscal Capacity, and China’s Housing Cycle

with Chang Gao  ·  [Hide]

LGFV issuance spreads rise with local government leverage — under OLS, under a predetermined maturity-rollover instrument, and under the 2015 central-government debt swap. Housing-price growth is hump-shaped in leverage and turns negative past 18–23% of GDP. We read these patterns through a general-equilibrium model in which a leverage-dependent financial friction creates a debt threshold beyond which infrastructure investment collapses, so the bust needs no exogenous shock.

Rationed Entry and the Allocation of Talent

[Slides]  [Hide]

Some desirable jobs are not priced. They are rationed by quota and handed out by examination: in 2021 China’s civil service took about 20,000 people from 1.5 million applicants. This paper models entry into such a sector alongside wage-clearing sectors and an outside option. Admission rises with an applicant’s rank inside the pool of people who chose to apply, so the admission rate is an equilibrium object rather than a parameter. Neither instrument is monotone. A more generous outside option at first enlarges the queue, since it makes failing cheaper to risk; past a threshold it is simply taken instead. A larger prize at first improves selection and then dilutes it, as the marginal applicants arrive from further down the ability distribution. The two humps have different engines: one works on the size of the queue and survives with the admission schedule held fixed, the other on who is in the pool. Because both instruments move that pool, they cannot be set apart from each other.
